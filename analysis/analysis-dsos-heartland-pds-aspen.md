# Analysis: Dental Service Organizations (Heartland, PDS Health, Aspen/TAG, Smile Brands, Dental Care Alliance, Tend) and franchising

A DSO (dental service organization) is a company, usually private-equity-owned, that runs the non-clinical side of many dental offices (billing, insurance, phones, purchasing, marketing, HR) under a management agreement, because most US states bar non-dentists from owning a practice or directing care. DSOs are Cyclical's economic thesis carried out with people and ownership instead of software: they pool the back office across 1,000–2,000 offices and run a central call center that is literally the "pooled exception desk" from realizations.md. **Overlap score: 4/10.** The economic logic is the same, but the mechanism is entirely different: buying practices, human shared services and point-AI vendors, with no outcome-graded agent loop, no eval stages and no graph. The most important lesson runs against Cyclical: **DSOs already pool office metrics across hundreds of offices, and the documented result is Goodhart at industrial scale.** Production quotas and hygienist sales bonuses led to attorney-general settlements in NY (2015), MA (2023) and CA (2026) and to overtreatment scandals that ended in bankruptcy (Small Smiles 2012, Benevis/Kool Smiles 2020). A pooled optimizer that touches anything near treatment decisions will be read through that history.

## What it actually is

**Market size and position.** About 14% of US dentists were DSO-affiliated in 2023, and more than 1 in 4 of those within 10 years of graduating (ADA Health Policy Institute, cited in realizations.md). Roughly 86% of dentists, mostly in independent practices, sit outside DSOs. That is Cyclical's addressable market, and it is the same market the DSOs are trying to acquire.

**What DSOs centralize.** Heartland's own description of its non-clinical support lists patient marketing, billing and accounts receivable, purchasing, scheduling support and patient communications. Its accounting team handles collections, credentialing, insurance claims and reporting. A "Patient Support Center" takes overflow calls for its offices and routes callbacks into their scheduling systems [5]. RingCentral's case study covers that contact center at 1,400 offices handling about a million inquiries a month (headline only; the page could not be fetched) [5]. The same pattern holds at every large DSO: the clinic keeps the dentist, assistants, hygienists and usually one or two front-desk staff, and the support center takes billing, insurance verification, credentialing, call overflow, recall campaigns, purchasing, HR/payroll and marketing.

**Measured economics** (consultant and vendor sources, directional, none audited) [11]:
- DSOs typically charge a management fee of **8–25% of collections**.
- Shared services (supply buying, payer negotiation, marketing) add **4–9 points of office EBITDA**, and group purchasing alone accounts for **3–6 points**.
- Office "four-wall" EBITDA (before corporate overhead) runs **25–32%**. Well-run DSOs land at **20–25%** after corporate overhead, and **corporate overhead of 10–16% of revenue** is the swing factor.
- Collections: the typical practice collects ~97% of net production. A vendor's claim that the top decile reaches 123% looks wrong and is excluded.
- **Leverage is the defining fact.** Moody's rated Heartland Caa1 in 2021 and upgraded it to B3 at ~7.0× debt/EBITDA. It affirmed B3 with a positive outlook in July 2025, and said a further upgrade needs leverage below 6.0×. Heartland also took a $200M incremental loan for acquisitions despite "leverage concerns." Net patient revenue was ~$1.5B in 2020 [6].

The PE thesis is mostly **multiple arbitrage**: buy single practices at ~4–7× EBITDA and sell a platform at ~12–15× (from memory). Operational centralization is the story that justifies the multiple. It is not, mainly, where the value comes from. Purchasing and payer contracting (scale bargaining) supply most of the measurable uplift. Back-office labor pooling supplies less, and nobody publishes a measured "collections uplift" from centralized RCM (revenue cycle management, the billing-to-payment pipeline).

**Franchising as the general analog.** A franchisor licenses a brand and an operating system and collects royalties of roughly 4–8% of revenue plus a pooled marketing fund (from memory). It benchmarks franchisees against each other and pushes a single operations manual. The franchisor's asset is the codified playbook and the brand. A franchisee buys the right to follow a proven process. Aspen Dental runs a quasi-franchise "ownership" path for its dentists (from memory), and partnership DSOs (MB2, Dental Care Alliance) let dentists keep equity (from memory). These are the formats closest to "keep your practice, get the pooled back office."

**A11, where DSOs are ahead.** realizations.md says testing role removal "needs hundreds of clinics split at random." A single top DSO has that internally and can randomize staffing by decree, with no consent problem across separate owners. Heartland's ~1,900 offices are about 19% of the 10,000-clinic model in realizations.md. On pure pooling volume, the top five DSOs are self-sufficient pools, so Cyclical's pooling moat is weakest against them and strongest against the ~86% of dentists outside DSOs and against DSOs under ~100 offices. No DSO, however, is found pooling *graded agent outcomes* or running randomized experiments. They pool labor and compare KPIs. The capability is latent, not used.

**A12, the failure Cyclical must design against.** Office benchmarking turns into quotas. In 2015 the NY Attorney General found that an Aspen internal document told hygienists who were short of revenue targets to sell more products and services, after 300+ consumer complaints since 2005. The settlement barred Aspen's management company from patient-care decisions in New York [14]. California's May 2026 settlement alleges per-sale hygienist bonuses ($50 per new patient sale, $100 per existing patient sale) as corporate practice of dentistry, with $2M in penalties and $300K in restitution [13]. Massachusetts settled for $3.5M in 2023 over "free" services that were then billed [13]. Earlier, Small Smiles (Church Street Health Management, 67 centers) and Kool Smiles (Benevis) faced Medicaid overtreatment cases involving children (from memory: Small Smiles paid a ~$24M False Claims Act settlement in 2010, Benevis ~$24M in 2018). Both later went bankrupt: Church Street in 2012 with $303M of debt, Benevis in 2020 [15]. The lesson is the exact pattern Cyclical's "claims agent downcodes to raise acceptance" example warns about, at network scale: **a pooled optimizer amplifies whatever its local metric rewards, and in dentistry regulators treat revenue pressure on clinical nodes as illegal, not just as bad.**

**A2/A3, observation has legal exposure.** *Lisota v. Heartland Dental*, a proposed class action, alleged that AI transcribed and analyzed patient calls to Heartland offices without consent. It was dismissed with prejudice on July 24, 2026 (single secondary source; verify) [16]. Reading the logs of tools people already use is Cyclical's onboarding method, and when those logs are phone calls, wiretap and consent law applies (two-party-consent states such as California). The dismissal is encouraging but not a safe harbor.

## Where they're ahead of Cyclical

1. **Authority to restructure.** A DSO owns the P&L and can remove a role, centralize billing or change staffing templates across 1,000 offices in a quarter. Cyclical's orchestrator only *proposes* to a clinic owner. The DSO's reorganizations are real, Cyclical's are suggestions.
2. **Pooled volume inside one decision-maker.** No data-sharing agreements are needed across owners, and randomized assignment across offices is possible today.
3. **A working pooled human exception desk.** Heartland's Patient Support Center and central billing teams already exist and have been tuned for years. They are the human baseline Cyclical's central desk (~0.1 FTE per clinic) has to beat.
4. **Scale bargaining.** Purchasing (3–6 EBITDA points) and payer-contract leverage are value Cyclical cannot replicate with software. Those are the biggest measured uplifts, and they are outside Cyclical's loop entirely.
5. **Recruiting and debt-free entry for young dentists.** This is why 1 in 4 recent graduates affiliate. Software offers them nothing comparable.

## Where they're doing it differently (and whether their way is better)

**Ownership vs integration.** DSOs solve the "system-of-record" problem (A4's unresolved system-node question, A14's strategic risk) by owning the office and forcing a single practice-management system. Cyclical must integrate with whatever each clinic runs. The DSO way is better for data quality and worse for reach: it costs an acquisition per office and caps out at the ~14% of dentists willing to sell or affiliate.

**AI adoption order.** The large DSOs bought **clinical imaging AI first** (VideaHealth at Heartland 2024 and Aspen 2026, Pearl at PDS 2026, Overjet at Sonrava and Imagen), **eligibility/RCM AI second** (DentalXChange at Heartland, Apr 2026), and **front-desk voice agents last and piecemeal** [1][2][4][7][10]. The reason is incentives. Imaging AI raises diagnosed treatment per visit, which is revenue, without touching anyone's job, while front-office agents cut into the DSO's own central workforce and its outsourced call-center contracts. Cyclical targets the front office first (the 9→6 staff model). That is the opposite order, and it fits independents, where front-office labor is the owner's own cost. Be aware that imaging AI marketed as "finds 37% more disease" (Pearl, vendor claim) [4] is exactly the clinical-revenue lever the regulators above are watching.

**Point vendors vs graph.** DSOs run a multi-vendor stack (imaging AI + eligibility AI + RingCentral + SAP + Salesforce + PMS), each contracted and measured separately. That stack has no cross-step guardrails: nobody grades the eligibility agent on downstream collections. Cyclical's graph is better here, and the fragmentation is the opening.

**Benchmarking as management pressure vs benchmarking as learning.** DSOs compare offices to rank them and pay bonuses, and the benchmark is the incentive. Cyclical uses pooled outcomes to grade *processes* (which confirmation text gets more show-ups) rather than people. Cyclical's version is better *if* it keeps the guardrail separation. The DSO record shows how fast "learning" becomes "quota" once compensation attaches.

## Top-level: why they chose this path, and what to learn

**Starting asset and constraint.** DSOs started from capital (PE debt plus equity) and the arbitrage between small-practice and platform multiples. The binding constraint was the corporate-practice-of-dentistry doctrine: a non-dentist can't own or direct care, so they built a management-company structure that legally controls only non-clinical work. With no software leverage in 1997–2020, centralization meant moving human work to a support center, so the "pooled back office" is a call center and billing floor. Debt (~6–7× EBITDA) forces short hold periods (4–7 years), which favors buying vendor tools that show EBITDA within a year over building multi-year outcome infrastructure.

**Customer, competitor, or proof? All three, by segment.**
- **Proof:** Centralizing billing, verification, phones and recall across hundreds of offices works well enough to support a ~14% share of dentists and B-rated debt. The proof is weaker than realizations.md implies, though. The measurable uplift is mostly purchasing and payer scale, and back-office pooling has no published collections-uplift number. Cyclical should not cite "DSO economics" as evidence that *agent* pooling pays. It proves that *centralization* pays.
- **Competitor:** For independent dentists, joining a DSO is the main alternative to Cyclical. Cyclical's pitch is "the DSO back office without selling your practice or paying an 8–25%-of-collections management fee." On a $1.5M-collections office that fee is $120–375K a year, against Cyclical's ~$5–10K agent bill (rough), a very large price umbrella. DSOs also compete for the same practices through acquisition.
- **Customer:** The best customers are **mid-size DSOs (20–200 offices)**: already centralized (so the graph is legible), on one PMS (so A2 observation is easy), too small to build, and needing outside pooling for rare segments. Large DSOs (1,000+) will buy point tools and could be acquirers.

**Would a DSO build Cyclical internally?** A top-5 DSO has the pool, the standardized processes and the authority to randomize. What it lacks is engineering depth (its tech teams are integration and procurement shops running SAP, RingCentral and Salesforce), a clean event log (acquired offices on mixed systems), and the incentive horizon (PE holds). The 2025–26 record is consistent: every large DSO announcement found was a vendor deployment, none was an in-house agent platform [1][2][4][7][10][12]. Rough probability that a top-5 DSO builds a general graph/eval/orchestrator internally by 2029: ~15%. More likely, a large DSO standardizes on one vendor platform that grows into this. Overjet is the candidate, since it already spans clinical AI, claim intelligence and voice across many DSOs, and pools across them as a vendor.

**Copy:**
1. The central exception desk is real and already exists as Heartland's Patient Support Center [5]. Price and staff Cyclical's desk against it.
2. PortalPass-style central credential management [2] is a concrete answer to part of A4's system-node question: a designated custodian node holds system credentials and issues scoped, logged access.
3. Sell to mid-size DSOs first as a beachhead: one buyer, many offices, one PMS, and a human ops leader who already wants an orchestrator view.

**Avoid:**
1. Pooled revenue KPIs flowing down to clinical staff. Hard-fence clinical nodes (diagnosis, treatment planning, hygienist recommendations) out of the optimizer's reward. Pair every revenue metric with a clinical-harm and complaint guardrail (A12). The AG settlements show that regulators read "corporate system influences treatment" as corporate practice of dentistry.
2. Call recording and analysis without explicit consent, given *Lisota* [16].
3. Vendor-exclusive lock-in (A13). DSOs are signing "exclusive AI partner" deals now [10], and Cyclical's model-agnosticism is the counter-position.

**Exploit:**
1. DSOs' multi-vendor stacks have no cross-step guardrails, so the graph-level eval is the wedge.
2. Front-office agents are what large DSOs are slowest to adopt, because it cuts their own central workforce. Independents feel that cost directly.
3. Dentist resentment of DSO quotas makes "keep ownership, get pooling" a sellable message to the ~86% who haven't joined.

**Threat level: medium-low now, rising.** The signal that it is rising: a top DSO, or Overjet on behalf of several DSOs, announces (a) cross-office *randomized* experiments on staffing or workflow, or (b) outcome-graded agents in RCM/front desk that graduate by case type, or (c) offers its support-center-plus-AI stack *as a service to non-affiliated practices* ("DSO-as-a-service"). Item (c) would make a DSO a direct Cyclical competitor with an existing pool.
